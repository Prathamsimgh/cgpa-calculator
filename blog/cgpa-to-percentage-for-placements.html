CGPA to Percentage for Placements: What HR Actually Checks

Your transcript shows CGPA. The application form asks for percentage. Get the conversion wrong and you're either filtering yourself out — or misrepresenting yourself in.

Do companies ask for CGPA or percentage?

Most Indian recruiters — especially in campus placements — ask for percentage on application forms, even when your university only issues CGPA. The reasons are mundane: their applicant tracking systems were built around percentages, eligibility cutoffs ("60% throughout") are expressed in percentages, and HR teams compare candidates across universities. A few companies (and most foreign universities) accept CGPA directly, but you should assume percentage is the default ask.

The cutoff culture makes accuracy critical. A "60% and above" criterion means very different CGPAs at different universities: 6.0 under plain ×10, but 6.75 under VTU's (CGPA − 0.75) × 10 formula. Students who convert with the wrong formula either miss cutoffs they actually clear, or clear cutoffs they actually miss — the second error being far more dangerous.

Why the official formula is non-negotiable

Large employers run background verification, and education verification routinely includes confirming that the percentage on your resume matches the university's official conversion. A VTU 2018-scheme student reporting 85% for an 8.5 CGPA (the generic ×10 figure) instead of the official 77.5% isn't making a rounding error — on paper, it's a 7.5-point inflation of a credential. Reported cases of offer revocation over exactly this kind of mismatch exist, and they're entirely avoidable: convert with your university's formula, and the number defends itself.

The tempting mistake is rationalising that "everyone uses ×10." Everyone doesn't — and the verification vendor checking your file uses the university's published rule, not campus lore.

Government and PSU applications: stricter still

UPSC, SSC, banking, and PSU applications typically require the percentage derived from your university's official formula, stated explicitly in the notification. Several PSUs (ONGC, BHEL, SAIL, NTPC are commonly cited) accept the converted percentage — but some ask for a letter from your university's academic section certifying the conversion formula. If your university uses a non-standard formula, arrange that letter before the application deadline; registrars are not known for speed.

Studying abroad: the conversion certificate

Foreign universities and credential evaluators like WES evaluate Indian transcripts course-by-course rather than trusting a single converted percentage. Still, application portals ask for both CGPA and percentage. Get a "conversion formula certificate" from your registrar stating the official mapping — it preempts the most common document query in the process and is specifically what WES-style evaluations want alongside your transcripts.

A placement-season checklist

  1. Confirm your formula now — from your marksheet back or exam cell, not from a random calculator. Note the regulation/scheme year.
  2. Compute once, use everywhere. Put the same percentage on your resume, LinkedIn, and every application form. Inconsistency across documents is itself a red flag.
  3. Keep the workings. Save the formula reference (a photo of the marksheet back works) with your placement documents.
  4. Get the certificate if your formula is unusual. VTU, AKTU, Mumbai University students especially — a registrar's letter costs little and settles everything.
  5. Never round up across a cutoff. 59.6% is not 60%. Cutoffs are thresholds, not estimates.

The 60% cutoff, translated

"60% throughout" is the most common eligibility line in Indian campus hiring. Here's what it demands in CGPA under each major formula — because the number you need to beat depends entirely on your university:

  • Plain ×10: 60% → 6.00 CGPA
  • CBSE ×9.5: 60% → 6.32 CGPA
  • (CGPA − 0.5) × 10 (GTU): 60% → 6.50 CGPA
  • (CGPA − 0.75) × 10 (VTU 2018/AKTU): 60% → 6.75 CGPA
  • Mumbai University 7.1×+11: 60% → 6.90 CGPA

A VTU student with 6.5 CGPA clears "60%" under a generic conversion but misses it under their official formula (57.5%). Meanwhile a Mumbai University student needs nearly 6.9. If a cutoff is make-or-break for you, convert it into CGPA terms with your official formula and compare against your actual CGPA — that single check has saved more placement seasons than any resume tip.

What verification vendors actually do

When a company runs a background check, the education-verification step typically confirms three things with your university: that you earned the degree, the CGPA/grade on record, and — where the vendor is thorough — the institution's official conversion formula. They then check whether the percentage on your resume is consistent with that formula. This is a mechanical comparison, not a judgment call: 77.5% computed from an 8.5 VTU CGPA passes; 85% from the same CGPA gets flagged as a discrepancy, and the report goes back to HR with the word "mismatch" in it. The vendors don't care which formula is "fairer." They care which one is official.

Resume and LinkedIn consistency

One underrated source of trouble: reporting different numbers in different places — 77.5% on the resume, "8.5 CGPA" on LinkedIn, 78% on the application portal. Each is defensible alone; together they look sloppy at best. Pick one representation per platform convention (percentage on resumes and forms, CGPA on LinkedIn education entries is the common split), make sure they're mutually consistent under your official formula, and never let a portal's auto-conversion overwrite your verified number without checking what formula it used.

Already submitted a wrong number? Fix it now

If reading this made you realise your resume carries a generically-converted percentage, don't panic — fix it before verification starts. Recompute with your official formula, update the resume and every portal, and if you've already cleared interviews, proactively tell the recruiter: "I noticed my percentage was computed with a generic formula; the correct figure per my university's regulation is X." A self-correction before the background check reads as diligence. A discrepancy discovered by the background check reads as misrepresentation. The same facts, opposite interpretations — timing is everything.
